How to Read the Fine Print on Supplement Subscription Trials

Supplement subscription trials look generous: a free bottle, a first month at half price, a trial for the cost of postage. The generosity usually ends in the fine print, where the trial converts into a full-price subscription that is easy to join and hard to leave. Reading that fine print before you sign up takes five minutes and saves months of charges.

The first thing to find is the conversion date. A trial that lasts fourteen days converts on day fifteen — and the first full-price charge lands the same day. Mark the date the moment you sign up, and set a reminder two days before it. Brands count on you forgetting; a calendar reminder is the cheapest protection there is.

The second thing is the price after the trial. The trial price is rarely the subscription price. Look for the full monthly charge, the shipping cost, and whether the price is locked or can rise. A trial that converts into a subscription at twice the trial price, with shipping added, is a different purchase from the one advertised.

The third thing is the cancellation route. Can you cancel from your account, or must you email, or phone during business hours? A subscription that is easy to join and hard to leave is a trap, not a service. Test the cancellation path before you need it: log in, find the cancel button, and note where it is. If there is no cancel button at all, walk away.

The fourth thing is the minimum term. Some trials lock you into three or six months after the trial ends, with an early-exit fee. The fee is usually in the terms, in small print, next to the trial offer. A free trial with a six-month lock-in is not free — it is a six-month contract with a free first month.

Finally, check what the brand says about refunds. A trial that charges you in error should be refundable; a brand that refuses refunds for trial conversions is telling you how it treats its customers.
